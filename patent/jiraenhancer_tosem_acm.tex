\begin{abstract}
Human–AI collaboration in software engineering now comprises coding, requirements enhancement, planning, and controlled updates to collaborative engineering artifacts, but unmanaged automated changes introduce severe risks to project governance and data integrity. We introduce Jira Enhancer, a system that converts sparsely specified epics into reviewable implementation stories while leaving human reviewers in complete control of issue-tracker state updates. The method combines context-aware evidence gathering across distributed repositories, hierarchical decomposition, automated refinement based on machine confidence tracking, bounded prompt-policy selection, explicit human approval gates, and conflict-safe writeback transactions. We evaluate authenticated field evidence from an industrial deployment at Oracle India Pvt. Ltd., tracking a team of 40 professional engineers across 14 large-scale epics, alongside an artifact evaluation blind to the source evidence, a matched baseline control experiment, a controlled fault-injection suite comprising 72,000 observations.

In two deployment phases, we recorded 70 terminal story endpoints: 69 implementation stories were developed, while a duplicate-safe matching pass successfully identified and linked one pre-existing record. In the matched baseline study, five independent reviewers evaluated 42 anonymized requirement packages created using 14 common inputs by a deterministic template baseline (B1), a one-shot drafting control (B2), and Jira Enhancer (B3). An exploratory analysis of the corrected reviewer ratings uses a revised composite emphasizing implementation readiness, dependencies and risks, and granularity and actionability. B3 scored 4.32 versus 3.59 for B2 on the 1--5 scale, a paired difference of 0.74 points (95\% epic-bootstrap CI [0.55, 0.93], Holm-adjusted exact $p=0.00024$). B3 exceeded B1 by 1.76 points (95\% CI [1.43, 2.10], $p=0.00024$) and scored higher than both baselines on all 14 epics. B3 received Ready-or-Minor-revision classifications in 88.1\% of primary evaluations, compared with 66.7\% for B2, with 5 versus 14 Major-revision ratings. Requirement assessment time was 2.15 minutes lower for B3 (95\% CI [$-2.35$, $-1.99$], $p=0.00024$). In a separate three-packet diagnostic, two blinded model evaluators recorded 0 unsupported additions and 0 missing source requirements for B3, compared with 4 and 13 findings for B2; ambiguous acceptance-criterion findings were 4 versus 21. These are summed model-evaluator findings. In independent fault-injection trials, B3's source-bound transaction envelope blocked all injected unsafe mutations while permitting all eligible writes under the declared fault model. Together, the evaluations support higher weighted requirement-quality ratings, a more favorable revision profile, and controlled writeback, with a higher computational budget than one-shot drafting.

\end{abstract}

\section{INTRODUCTION}
The topic of this research paper addresses the problem of human-AI cooperation in software engineering: how AI agents can transform incomplete planning intent into reviewable and execution-ready Jira stories while maintaining accountability, governance, and control over shared engineering records. The problem under consideration is associated with well-known requirements-engineering issues, including ambiguity, incompleteness, and stakeholder coordination. \textbf{These core issues present a major challenge in software engineering because incomplete tickets force engineering leads to waste time manually hunting for missing data across separate repositories, slowing down sprint execution cycles.} Teams do not need another channel that produces fluent text which must then be reconciled manually. They need support that fits how planning decisions are reviewed, challenged, approved, and audited. \textbf{Maintaining strict human authority and governance over shared engineering records is vital because any incorrect automated writeback to a project tracker instantly corrupts a team's core development roadmap, causing broken dependencies and task inflation.}

This problem involves machine reasoning, requirements engineering, software delivery operations, and enterprise governance. It is particularly relevant to organizations that want AI assistance but do not accept opaque automation, uncontrolled writeback, or weak accountability.

Recent software-engineering roadmaps also describe agentic systems as collaborators across the full development process, rather than only as code generators, and they stress trust, human values, and verifiable evaluation. We therefore treat Jira Enhancer as an AI-supported collaborative planning workflow rather than as a generator. The AI provides contextual information, decomposition suggestions, confidence estimates, and draft revisions. Human reviewers evaluate the evidence, approve or decline proposed stories, and retain ultimate control over external issue-tracker mutation. Such a framing is consistent with human-AI interaction literature that recommends systems reveal their capabilities, uncertainty, and user control at the point of decision. It also matches conclusions from studies of automation trust that emphasize the importance of appropriate rather than blind reliance on AI.

\subsection{Research Scope and Research Questions}
AI-supported planning is considered not as one-shot automation but as a joint socio-technical workflow. The object of study is the collaboration boundary: which stages of epic decomposition can be delegated to an AI agent, which stages require human decision-making, how confidence and evidence should be represented, and how shared software-engineering records can be changed without reducing accountability.

\textbf{Our research focus centers on Epics and the Jira platform because they serve as the foundational source of truth where project requirements are defined and prioritized in commercial engineering environments. By evaluating specific variables such as machine confidence, evidence provenance, and programmatic policy gates, we seek to determine if a structured AI assistant can lower developer review burden without introducing duplication errors or stale data writes.} Therefore, we investigate four research questions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1:} How can a human-AI workflow split underspecified software epics into reviewable implementation stories without compromising human authority over acceptance and writeback?
    \item \textbf{RQ2:} How can machine confidence, evidence provenance, and policy gates be combined to help reviewers determine whether AI-generated planning records are reliable enough for action?
    \item \textbf{RQ3:} What failure modes emerge when agentic planning systems interact with enterprise issue trackers, including missing evidence, duplicate story creation, partial writeback failure, and stale approvals?
    \item \textbf{RQ4:} How does a governed human-AI workflow perform, relative to executable template/rule and generic-LLM baselines, on decomposition quality, review effort, and implementation readiness?
\end{itemize}

The questions consider effective collaboration, joint performance, persistent agents, and new failure modes in software-engineering workflows. The contribution extends beyond implementation of the tool. It presents an executable study of how AI agents and human reviewers coordinate around real software-engineering records under explicit governance constraints \textbf{within a live enterprise footprint at Oracle India Pvt. Ltd., tracking the workflow behavior of 40 professional software practitioners across 14 large-scale epics.}

\section{RELATED WORK AND SYSTEM MOTIVATION}
In modern software development, applications depend on an issue-tracking tool, such as Jira, to support execution. Although teams can capture high-level epics, these epics may be underspecified because they lack acceptance criteria, dependencies, and uniform estimation granularity. Previous work on agile requirements~\cite{R63,R64}, requirements standards~\cite{R62}, and issue-reporting quality~\cite{R53} shows that structure, completeness, and actionability greatly influence subsequent engineering processes~\cite{R52}. \textbf{In typical industrial landscapes, existing project management extensions and requirements tools generally fall into one of three distinct classes:}
\begin{itemize}
    \item static templates with no intelligence, \textbf{which establish standard formatting layouts but do not dynamically improve text completeness or semantic quality~\cite{R63,R62};}
    \item recommendation tools with no collaboration semantics or governance (high risk), \textbf{such as unconstrained large language model copilots that generate fluent prose but lack internal confidence validation loops~\cite{R60,R59}; or}
    \item \textbf{automated workflow automation engines that route issues deterministically based on simple rules but are completely} unable to correlate confidence, review decisions, and write permissions (low trust).
\end{itemize}

This shortcoming is observed in everyday practice when doing simple things such as re-writing tickets during sprint planning, inferring hidden dependencies from distributed documentation by engineering leads, and dealing with unclear acceptance criteria by quality assurance during testing. \textbf{This makes a cooperative human-AI approach highly valuable, as an intelligent assistant can flag semantic gaps while a human developer guides the refinement loop to maintain process accountability.} The shortcoming is not limited to low process efficiency but rather to the inability to shape the information between capturing intentions and implementation. Similar issues were identified in the bug reporting and issue triage research with regards to vague terminology and poor routing signals as factors increasing coordination cost~\cite{R67,R68,R66}. Thus, the technical problem is to build a reliable machine system that can:
(1) infer missing stories from an epic,
(2) fill out stories until they become ready for implementation,
(3) estimate confidence and uncertainty,
(4) approve and enforce policies,
(5) write results deterministically and auditably.

As a system designed to work in the real world has to deal with partial evidence, limited access to external systems, and multiple users editing things simultaneously, it should be able to run reliably both with clean data and with the enterprise noise.

\subsection{Background from Existing Systems}
The proposed solution will help solve shortcomings of the related systems of three classes. First, static authoring templates in project-management software offer better formatting but do not significantly enhance requirements completeness and decomposition quality in general. Second, advanced large language model copilots generate fluent texts but frequently lack formal confidence controls, policy coupling, and conflict-safe mutation policies. At last, automated workflow engines may handle deterministic state transitions, however, rarely employ prompt-based approaches or construct stories from evidence. These systems alone cannot help humans and AI cooperate in order to share context, manage uncertainty, recover from failure, and retain responsibility for co-created software-engineering records.

LLM and generative AI literature in software engineering and requirements engineering show good results for requirements engineering, issue resolution, and development workflows~\cite{R60,R59}. At the same time, there are challenges in reliability, controllability, and evaluations of the existing approaches~\cite{R60,R59}. Research on code generation assistants describes conditions showing a similar pattern: developers inspect, revise, and place generated outputs within the task context rather than accepting them unchanged~\cite{R77,R78}. In case of agile planning, decomposition quality and review effort determine execution predictability, but governance-aware human oversight is still required for trustworthy deployments~\cite{R56,R52,R58}.

Multi-armed bandits and reinforcement learning algorithms are popular methods for selecting policies in an online setting under uncertainty~\cite{R84,R86,R100}. Application of these algorithms to enterprise planning is difficult due to delayed and potentially unreliable rewards and the need to adhere to governance constraints. In turn, the suggested approach solves this problem by coupling an adaptive prompt policy to an auditable workflow state and explicit approval gates.

Transaction safety~\cite{R88} and deterministic workflow control~\cite{R101} are important aspects of writeback safety in LLM-based code generation. They are considered in LLM-based retrieval and tool-usage pipelines. Combining these aspects with RL-guided, LLM-based decomposition is intended to increase planning intelligence while preserving mutation safety in shared issue trackers.

Thus, the proposed approach cannot be considered solely a code generation advancement but rather its unique combination of search, decomposition, confidence scoring, policy evaluation, approval semantics, and safe writeback.

\subsection{Related Work: Thematic Positioning}
\textbf{Theme A: Evidence retrieval and context construction.} Studies on retrieval-augmented generation and dense retrieval~\cite{R3,R32} show that evidence quality and the retrieval process are essential elements for response quality and hallucinations reduction~\cite{R22,R4,R6,R21}. Jira Enhancer incorporates this insight in the form of context packing: rather than delivering evidence blocks to the generator, Jira Enhancer ranks the evidence and maps missing signals.

\textbf{Theme B: Quality of reasoning via bounded refinement.} Studies on reasoning-prompting and self-reflection~\cite{R30,R14} show that it has positive impact when models are capable of deliberating, evaluating their outputs, and refining them according to some explicit criteria~\cite{R91,R89,R18}. In line with this concept, Jira Enhancer uses a rubric-based critic along with a limited amount of retries. In other words, improvements occur according to explicitly defined scoring dimensions and not infinite prompting.

\textbf{Theme C: Confidence, alignment, and reliable deployment.} Work on alignment shows that fluent output alone is not a necessary condition of reliable performance in an important workflow context, without confidence controls and human interaction~\cite{R90,R40,R13}. Furthermore, work in human-AI interaction and automation stresses the need for appropriate levels of trust, mental models, automation, and human control~\cite{R56,R57,R76,R75}. Jira Enhancer resolves this issue by tying confidence to the policy gates and requiring approval before performing any ticket mutation.

\textbf{Theme D: Constrained tools and safe execution.} Studies on LLM tool use~\cite{R16} demonstrate that models become more reliable when their outputs go through a constrained interface and verifiable tool steps~\cite{R91}. Jira Enhancer implements this approach with deterministic writeback, which includes hashing, idempotency, and limited field modifications.

\section{PROPOSED METHODOLOGY}
\textbf{The complete human-AI collaboration pipeline is structured as an interactive, state-guided loop designed to safely elevate requirements actionability within real-world engineering environments. The technical method is split into five functional layers that process an under-specified corporate epic, enrich its technical contents, and cross-examine it against organizational policy boundaries before executing any tracker updates.} The closed loop is made up of five layers: (1) scope resolution maps issue, sprint, epic, or release scopes to issue sets; (2) semantic enrichment develops structured descriptions, acceptance criteria, risks, dependencies, and next steps; (3) prompt selection chooses bounded refinement strategies and records attempt scores; (4) policy and governance apply thresholds, flags, and human-approval rules; and (5) writeback and audit perform bounded mutations with conflict checks and immutable traces. \textbf{The representative strengths, enterprise constraints, and theoretical position of Jira Enhancer relative to these multi-paradigm baseline configurations are summarized systematically in Table~\ref{tab:related-work-comparative}.}

First, the system generates stories, refines them toward a displayed confidence target (for example, \textbf{the target interface metric $C_{10}(s) \geq 9$, which scale-transforms normalized algorithm confidence $C(s) \in [0, 1]$ into a readable $0$--$10$ metric via $C_{10}(s) = 10C(s)$}), and creates a candidate story only after individual approval through controlled field mappings. \textbf{The system's internal creation gate threshold is configured strictly at $\tau_c = 0.9$ (displayed as $C_{10} = 9$ on the user interface scale). This conservative choice serves as a corporate quality constraint, ensuring a candidate story exhibits a near-complete array of source evidence parameters before it is permitted to consume an engineer's review attention.} The AI retrieves evidence, drafts and scores candidate stories, and performs bounded refinement. The reviewer evaluates the context and risk but retains final control. Generation, review, and writeback share a common state rather than being distinct processes, consistent with socio-technical guidelines for technology assessment in context~\cite{R71,R58}. The candidate is a stateful planning artifact, not just a disposable solution. The log contains the evidence, the choice of prompt arm, the variation in confidence, the intervention of the reviewer, and the policy decisions. Creative generation can be retried, but any mutation is permanent and limited by the policy decision for conflicts. This means that normal structuring can proceed while maintaining auditability of human for significant mutations.

\subsection{API and UI Console Workflow}\label{sec:workflow-ui}

\subsubsection{Workflow API}
The API supports two connected tasks. The first task enriches existing Jira items through a human review and revision loop. The second task decomposes an epic into candidate stories. Jira mutation remains separate from both tasks.

\paragraph{Run creation and inspection.}
\texttt{POST /api/v1/runs} starts an enrichment run for an issue, sprint, epic, or release. \texttt{GET /api/v1/runs/\allowbreak\{runId\}} returns the overall run status. \texttt{GET /api/v1/runs/\allowbreak\{runId\}/\allowbreak{}items/\allowbreak\{issueKey\}} returns the current draft. It also returns the evidence, warnings, readiness, confidence, policy state, and prompt-selection details. These read endpoints allow a reviewer to inspect the result before making a decision.

\paragraph{Human guidance and revision.}
The reviewer does not need to accept the first draft. \texttt{POST /api/v1/runs/\allowbreak\{runId\}/\allowbreak{}items/\allowbreak\{issueKey\}/\allowbreak{}approval} records a decision for one item. The allowed values are \texttt{approve}, \texttt{reject}, \texttt{regenerate}, \texttt{user\_input}, and \texttt{prompt}. The \texttt{user\_input} decision carries a human suggestion. The \texttt{prompt} decision carries a reviewer-written instruction for further modification. Both forms of guidance are sent back to the reasoning process, which produces a revised draft.

The run-level endpoint, \texttt{POST /api/v1/runs/\allowbreak\{runId\}/\allowbreak{}approval}, applies the same decision and guidance to every item in the run. The system may also ask a clarification question. In this case, \texttt{POST /api/v1/runs/\allowbreak\{runId\}/\allowbreak{}items/\allowbreak\{issueKey\}/\allowbreak{}interaction-answer} records the human answer and continues the revision. Human suggestions and follow-up prompts are therefore part of the API workflow.

\paragraph{Controlled writeback.}
\texttt{POST /api/v1/runs/\allowbreak\{runId\}/\allowbreak{}items/\allowbreak\{issueKey\}/\allowbreak{}writeback} writes one approved item after the policy, validation, source-conflict, and retry checks. \texttt{POST /api/v1/runs/\allowbreak\{runId\}/\allowbreak{}writeback} applies this operation across the run. Each item still receives its own result. This preserves item-level reasons for a successful, blocked, conflicting, or repeated request.

\paragraph{Epic story decomposition.}
\texttt{POST /api/v1/epics/\allowbreak\{epicKey\}/\allowbreak{}stories/\allowbreak{}suggest} reads the epic, existing child stories, and available evidence. It returns editable story candidates with readiness, confidence, threshold status, warnings, and evidence references. It does not create Jira stories.

\texttt{POST /api/v1/epics/\allowbreak\{epicKey\}/\allowbreak{}stories/\allowbreak{}create} receives the reviewed story payloads. It checks confidence and approval. It skips matching existing summaries and attempts to create only the remaining stories. Its response lists created and skipped items, including duplicate and creation-failure reasons.

The API therefore supports a full cycle: inspect, suggest, provide human guidance, revise, approve, and write back. Human users and controlled clients use the same contracts and governance checks.

\subsubsection{Console Workflow}
The console exposes the same workflow in three connected views. The enrichment view starts a run. It shows each Jira draft with its evidence, warnings, readiness, confidence, and selected prompt. The review view allows the user to approve, reject, request regeneration, provide a human suggestion, or submit a Jira-specific prompt for further modification. The same guidance can be applied to one Jira or to all Jira items in the run.

\begin{samepage}
The \textbf{Epic Story Breakdown} view provides the following features:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item epic-key and project-key inputs,
  \item the suggestion action, which reads the epic and proposes child stories,
  \item an editable structured payload for reviewing each story, with an \texttt{approved} flag per story,
  \item the approved-story creation action for writing back qualified rows.
\end{enumerate}
\end{samepage}

The reviewer can edit the generated content, add missing context, or provide a new prompt before approval. Writeback remains a separate action. This prevents a revision request from being treated as permission to change Jira.

Human-led and agent-led operations are both provided via the API. The human reviewer assesses the narrative and the possible risks through the console, whereas controlled agents or pipelines do that using the same endpoints according to deterministic payload contracts. This allows governed integration with release-planning bots, PM dashboards, and governance data lakes.

The UI focuses on ``decision visibility,'' giving the reviewer the sources of confidence, warnings, policy reason codes, approval provenance, reasons for skipping duplicates, and preserved retry selections for each story candidate. This discourages reviewers from treating confidence as an unsupported number and enables quicker and better accept, reject, or retry decisions. The design seeks appropriate trust rather than maximum automation, reflecting evidence that human--AI team performance depends on users' mental models of system behavior \cite{R56,R57,R76}.

\subsubsection{Evidence Availability and Recovery}\label{sec:evidence-recovery}

The system classifies warning codes, such as external authentication requirements and MCP unavailability, and propagates them to run- and story-level review records. This supports graceful degradation:
\begin{equation}
\texttt{service\_mode} \in \{\texttt{full-context},\texttt{reduced-context}\}.
\end{equation}
\textit{Full-context} mode is used for cases with external evidence; \textit{reduced-context} mode is applied when only issue-local evidence is available.

\textit{Reduced-context} mode is designed to remain useful instead of failing softly. It still provides structured drafts and warning metadata, which allows reviews to continue in an informed way.

In reliability engineering, the ``graceful capability collapse'' pattern is applied instead of binary failure. For example, if a high-value evidence source such as Confluence is offline, the system retains decomposition capability by using the available issue records and marking the missing evidence categories. Available issue records prevent planning deadlock, while metadata preservation maintains the meaning of the confidence score.

Transient failures are retried using exponential backoff and jitter, while terminal errors are reported with machine-actionable error codes. The technique allows for automatic retry management at the orchestration layer without hiding permanent integration issues.

The user-guided workflow maintains collaboration context through the stages in case of failures. If the story creation fails or times out, then the selected stories will still be present in the console. User will be able to look at the partial generation results in Jira and continue working on them without having to regenerate the decision set. Such capability is important for human--AI cooperation since recovery should not be limited to backend functionality but should be visible for a human making retry, change, or cancel decisions.

\subsection{Traceable Data Records}
Every run captures scope, requester, time stamps, and idempotency information along with item state, confidence, readiness, policy flags, structured drafts, references to external evidence, decisions and notes on approval, and before/after writeback hashes. Since each piece of information is a first class record, the planning decision behind it may be subsequently reconstructed for empirical evaluation~\cite{R70,R54}, auditing~\cite{R93}, or incident analysis. Immutable event streams maintain event ordering, whereas mutable snapshots facilitate low-latency querying. Informational warnings alert about non-critical deficiencies, advisory warnings bring problems to reviewer attention, and blocking warnings stop mutation until the issue gets fixed.

\subsection{Formal Problem Formulation}
Let $E$ be an epic, $S_{\text{existing}}$ the set of already-linked stories, $\mathcal{D}$ the available evidence corpus, and $\Pi$ a versioned governance policy. \textbf{To model practical industrial lifecycles cleanly, the comprehensive evidence corpus is split into separate components: $\mathcal{D} = \mathcal{D}_{\text{ext}} \cup \mathcal{D}_{\text{jira}}$. Here, $\mathcal{D}_{\text{ext}}$ represents externally pulled context sources, which concretely include Confluence technical design document pages, corporate requirements specifications, and active Bitbucket code pull-request log branches. Conversely, $\mathcal{D}_{\text{jira}}$ isolates localized requirements constraints, containing issue-embedded discussion histories and text architecture summaries recorded directly inside the core Jira ticket text description field.} This distinction is important because, when external links are unavailable but the epic description contains useful design rules, APIs, guardrails, or tests, the system can still produce evidence-grounded candidates while identifying their provenance as Jira-embedded context. The system constructs a finite candidate set $S_{\text{cand}}$ and selects a subset $S_{\text{new}} \subseteq S_{\text{cand}}$ for possible creation. Here, requirements are modeled as evolving and negotiated objects rather than fixed text~\cite{R51}. This agrees with established RE views that requirements are incomplete and shaped by stakeholders~\cite{R61}. It also follows requirements-quality guidance on clarity, verifiability, feasibility, and traceability~\cite{R62}. Each candidate $s$ is represented by the tuple
\begin{equation}
m(s) = (C(s), R(s), U(s), \text{Amb}(s), \rho(s), A(s), h_s) ,
\end{equation}
where $C(s)$ denotes normalized confidence, $R(s)$ denotes readiness, $U(s)$ is expected utility, $\text{Amb}(s)$ is ambiguity, $\rho(s)$ is risk, $A(s) \in \{0, 1\}$ is the binary human-approval indicator, and $h_s$ is the source-state cryptographic hash generated when the decision is formally made. The notation $A_s=A(s)$ denotes the same approval indicator in the algorithms below.

Normalized story identity is modeled through an equivalence relation $\sim_n$:
\begin{equation}
s_i \sim_n s_j \iff \operatorname{norm}(\operatorname{summary}(s_i)) =
\operatorname{norm}(\operatorname{summary}(s_j)),
\end{equation}
Here, $s_i$ and $s_j$ are indexed stories, $\operatorname{summary}(s)$ is the summary text of story $s$, and $\operatorname{norm}(\cdot)$ converts text to lowercase and strips all non-alphanumeric separators from it. The duplicate-free candidate domain, denoted as $\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dup}}$, is thus:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dup}}=\{s\in S_{\text{cand}}\mid \nexists s'\in S_{\text{existing}}: s\sim_n s'\}.
\end{equation}
Here, $s'$ ranges over existing stories. The above guarantees that work item generation will not lead to duplicates caused by normalization, which would make different versions of task summaries identical.

The basic feasibility relation is:
\begin{align}
S^* &= S_{\text{existing}} \cup S_{\text{new}}, \\
\forall s \in S_{\text{new}}:~ C(s) &\geq \tau_c, \\
\forall s \in S_{\text{new}}:~ A(s) &= 1 \text{ (approved)}, \\
S_{\text{eligible}} &= \{ s \in S_{\text{new}}\cap\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dup}} \mid C(s)\ge \tau_c \land A(s)=1 \land V(s)=1 \land \Pi(s)=\textsc{allow} \}, \\
\mathcal{W}_{\text{create}} &= \text{Create}(S_{\text{eligible}}),
\end{align}
Here, $S^*$ is the augmented plan, $S_{\text{eligible}}$ is the subset satisfying all creation conditions, and $\mathcal{W}_{\text{create}}$ is the result of applying the controlled creation operation $\text{Create}(\cdot)$ to that subset. The normalized creation threshold is $\tau_c\in[0,1]$ (default $\tau_c=0.9$). The displayed confidence is $C_{10}(s)=10C(s)$, so the default threshold corresponds to 9/10. The term
$V(s)\in\{0,1\}$ is the validation indicator for candidate story $s$.
It holds true (i.e. 1) only if the candidate passes schema, contradiction, and required field test. The symbol $\Pi$ denotes the versioned governance policy, and $\Pi(s)$ is its decision for candidate $s$. Condition $\Pi(s)=\textsc{allow}$ represents that the governing policy of the machine allows creation of the candidate.

This expression contains one of the important principles: high quality of generation does not necessarily guarantee the mutation. Creation is an action demanding both trust of the machine and of the human being, so that recommendation cannot become an action without permission.

Feasibility rules defined above specify which candidates are eligible for the creation phase. Yet, several of the candidates will satisfy all those rules and the machine still needs to determine which of them must enter the enhanced plan.

For each of the candidates $s\in S_{\text{cand}}$, we will define a binary decision variable $y_s\in\{0,1\}$, where $y_s=1$ if the candidate is selected and $y_s=0$ if it is not selected. With non-negative weights $\lambda_\rho$ and $\lambda_{\mathrm{Amb}}$ controlling the penalties for risk and ambiguity, the system uses the following objective:
\begin{equation}
	\max_{\{y_s\}} ~ \sum_{s \in S_{\text{cand}}} y_s
	\Big(
	U(s)-\lambda_\rho\rho(s)
	-\lambda_{\mathrm{Amb}}\operatorname{Amb}(s)
	\Big),
\end{equation}
The objective is subject to the following constraints, where $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ equals one when its condition is met and zero otherwise:
\begin{align}
	y_s &\leq \mathbb{1}[C(s)\ge \tau_c], \\
	y_s &\leq A(s), \\
	y_s &\leq V(s), \\
	y_s &\leq \mathbb{1}[\Pi(s)=\textsc{allow}], \\
	y_s &\leq 1-\mathbb{1}[\exists s'\in S_{\text{existing}}:
	\operatorname{norm}(\operatorname{summary}(s))
	=
	\operatorname{norm}(\operatorname{summary}(s'))], \\
	y_s &\in \{0,1\}.
\end{align}

Objective favours candidates with high expected utility $U(s)$. Selection score decreases if the candidate has high risk $\rho(s)$ or high ambiguity $\operatorname{Amb}(s)$.

Constraints make it possible to select a candidate only when its confidence level reaches $\tau_c$, it has human approval, it has passed validation, and active policy allows for its creation. The duplicate constraint makes it impossible to select a candidate whose normalized summary coincides with an existing story.

Thus, expected utility makes it possible to rank suitable candidates but cannot overcome confidence, approval, validation, policy, or duplicate conditions.

In scheduling applications, the objective can be extended with portfolio constraints:
\begin{align}
\sum_{s \in S_{\text{cand}}} y_s\,\mathrm{SP}(s) &\leq B_{\text{sprint}}, \\
\sum_{s \in S_{\text{cand}}} y_s\,\mathbb{1}[\operatorname{team}(s)=g] &\leq \operatorname{cap}_g, \\
\operatorname{dep}(s_i,s_j)=1 &\Rightarrow \operatorname{ord}(s_i) < \operatorname{ord}(s_j),
\end{align}
where $\mathrm{SP}(s)$ is the story-point estimate for candidate $s$ and $B_{\text{sprint}}$ is the sprint budget in story points. The function $\operatorname{team}(s)$ gives the team assigned to story $s$, $g$ identifies a team, and $\operatorname{cap}_g$ is its maximum number of selected stories. The dependency indicator $\operatorname{dep}(s_i,s_j)$ equals one when story $s_j$ depends on story $s_i$, and $\operatorname{ord}(s)$ gives the release-order position of story $s$. This demonstrates that the proposed mechanism can support both decomposition quality and downstream planning feasibility.

Two invariants follow directly from the constraints:
\begin{align}
y_s=1 &\Rightarrow C(s)\ge\tau_c, \\
y_s=1 &\Rightarrow A(s)=1 \land V(s)=1 \land \Pi(s)=\textsc{allow}.
\end{align}
Consequently, selected stories are both confidence-feasible and governance-feasible. These invariants support the multi-gate creation rule used later in the policy layer.
\paragraph{Proposition 1: eligibility soundness.}
For any candidate $s\in S_{\text{cand}}$, if $y_s=1$ in the constrained optimization problem above, then $s$ is confidence-feasible, approval-feasible, validation-feasible, policy-feasible, and duplicate-free:
\begin{equation}
y_s=1 \Rightarrow
C(s)\ge\tau_c \land A(s)=1 \land V(s)=1 \land \Pi(s)=\textsc{allow} \land s\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dup}}.
\end{equation}
\textit{Proof sketch.}
The implication follows by substituting $y_s=1$ into all five eligibility upper-bound constraints. Because $y_s$ is binary, every upper bound must evaluate to one; otherwise, the corresponding constraint would be violated. Hence, the confidence, approval, validation, policy, and duplicate constraints hold simultaneously.

\paragraph{Proposition 2: Mutation Requires Approval.}
If $A(s)=0$, then $s$ cannot be selected for creation under the optimization model:
\begin{equation}
A(s)=0 \Rightarrow y_s=0.
\end{equation}
\textit{Proof sketch.}
Approval condition says $y_s\le A(s)$. Given $A(s)=0$ and $y_s\in\{0,1\}$, the only feasible value is $y_s=0$. It establishes the human-authority invariant.

The system is set up for use in cases where confidence and approval will be the main factors involved, rather than label associated with the generated output.

\subsection{Readiness and Confidence Model}
\label{sec:readiness-confidence-model}

The system calculates readiness and confidence in four steps. It first records which evidence is available. It then calculates a base evidence score, adds a bounded contribution from generated content, and subtracts penalties for missing evidence. Finally, it converts the resulting readiness score into a confidence value.

Let $\mathbf{x}=(x_d,x_a,x_p,x_e,x_h,x_g)\in\{0,1\}^6$ represent the available evidence for story $s$:
\begin{align}
	x_d &= \mathbb{1}[\text{description exists}], \\
	x_a &= \mathbb{1}[\text{acceptance criteria exist}], \\
	x_p &= \mathbb{1}[\text{dependencies exist}], \\
	x_e &= \mathbb{1}[\text{external evidence available}], \\
	x_h &= \mathbb{1}[\text{activity history available}], \\
	x_g &= \mathbb{1}[\text{edge cases available}].
\end{align}
Each indicator equals one when the corresponding evidence is available and zero when it is missing.

The system uses these indicators to calculate the base evidence score, $R_{\text{src}}(\mathbf{x})$:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		R_{\text{src}}(\mathbf{x}) ={}& 0.25x_d + 0.25x_a + 0.15x_p \\
		&+ 0.15x_e + 0.10x_h + 0.10x_g.
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
The weights are positive values and add up to one. Therefore, $R_{\text{src}}(\mathbf{x})$ remains within $[0,1]$. The description and acceptance criteria have the highest weights since they provide the most crucial information required to evaluate the candidate story.

In create-run mode, the system can apply the bounded bonus $B_{\text{gen}}$ for the useful generated content:
\begin{equation}
	B_{\text{gen}}=\sum_i w_i z_i,
\end{equation}
where $i$ indexes quality aspects of the generated text, like the problem statement, acceptance criteria, risk model, or non-functional requirements, and $z_i\in[0,1]$ is the score for aspect $i$. Its weight is $w_i\geq0$, and the sum of the weights satisfies $\sum_i w_i\leq b_{\max}$, where $b_{\max}$ is the maximum permitted generated-content bonus. This upper limit will prevent excessive bonuses being applied to the generated text without supportive evidence.

The system imposes a gap penalty $P_{\text{gap}}$ for the important pieces of missing evidence:
\begin{equation}
	\begin{aligned}
		P_{\text{gap}} ={}& \eta_d(1-x_d)+\eta_a(1-x_a) \\
		&+\eta_p(1-x_p)+\eta_e(1-x_e)+\cdots .
	\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, the non-negative coefficients $\eta_d$, $\eta_a$, $\eta_p$, and $\eta_e$ weight the penalties for missing description, acceptance criteria, dependencies, and external evidence, respectively. The rest of the terms will be similar for other missing evidence.

The system calculates the readiness by combining the base score, bonus for the generated content, and penalty for the missing evidence:
\begin{align}
	R(s) &= \operatorname{clip}
	\bigl(R_{\text{src}}(\mathbf{x})+B_{\text{gen}}-P_{\text{gap}},0,0.99\bigr), \\
	C(s) &= \operatorname{clip}
	\bigl(R(s)+\delta_C,0,\gamma_{\text{ceiling}}\bigr).
\end{align}
In this case, the expression $\operatorname{clip}(u,a,b)=\min(\max(u,a),b)$ restricts the input value $u$ to the lower bound $a$ and upper bound $b$. The expression $\delta_C$ represents a bounded confidence modification. The graphical user interface shows the confidence as $C_{10}(s)=10C(s)$ and truncates it. The evidence-based confidence ceiling $\gamma_{\text{ceiling}}\in[0,1]$ limits confidence when there is not enough evidence, even if the draft is coherent.

In order to see the effect of evidence on readiness, we can also use the notation $R_s(\mathbf{x})$ for the readiness of story $s$ as a function of its evidence vector. If the bonus and penalty are constant, adding more evidence cannot decrease the readiness measure. Therefore,
\begin{equation}
	\mathbf{x}'\geq\mathbf{x}
	\quad\Rightarrow\quad
	R_s(\mathbf{x}')\geq R_s(\mathbf{x}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}'$ is a second evidence vector, and $\mathbf{x}'\geq\mathbf{x}$ means that every evidence indicator in $\mathbf{x}'$ is equal to or greater than the corresponding indicator in $\mathbf{x}$.

\paragraph{Lemma 1: confidence is bounded.}
For any generated candidate $s$, $R(s)\in[0,0.99]$ and $C(s)\in[0,\gamma_{\text{ceiling}}]$.
\textit{Proof sketch.}
Both values are calculated using a clipping operator. By definition, $\operatorname{clip}(u,a,b)$ belongs to $[a,b]$. Thus, $R(s)$ lies in $[0,0.99]$ and $C(s)$ lies in $[0,\gamma_{\text{ceiling}}]$, irrespective of the raw evidence score, generated bonus, or gap penalty.

\paragraph{Lemma 2: monotonicity under fixed penalties.}
For $\mathbf{x}',\mathbf{x}\in\{0,1\}^6$ such that $\mathbf{x}'\ge\mathbf{x}$ componentwise, $R_s(\mathbf{x}')\ge R_s(\mathbf{x})$ if $B_{\text{gen}}$, $P_{\text{gap}}$, and $\gamma_{\text{ceiling}}$ are fixed.
\textit{Proof sketch.}
The evidence score is calculated as a non-negative weighted sum over $\mathbf{x}$, with all weights being non-negative. Hence, $R_{\text{src}}(\mathbf{x}')\ge R_{\text{src}}(\mathbf{x})$. Applying the same generated bonus and penalty, followed by a monotone clipping function, maintains this inequality.

In practice, the blended formalism shows reviewers how the confidence score was calculated. This allows them to understand whether a particular score is low because of missing acceptance criteria, insufficient evidence, or complex dependencies.

\paragraph{Confidence scope.}
In this study, confidence is a bounded decision-support score based on the available evidence. It is not a probability of successful delivery. External mutation still requires validation, policy checks, and explicit human approval. Human approval remains the final authorization decision. Therefore, the study does not use confidence to authorize mutation by itself and does not evaluate probabilistic calibration.

Additionally, the model accounts for context-dependent penalties. For example, unresolved architectural dependencies add an uncertainty term to $P_{\text{gap}}$ even when the corresponding fields are populated. This approach helps avoid high confidence for tickets that appear complete but remain semantically weak, a concern reported in agile requirements, requirements-smell, and issue-report quality research \cite{R65,R52,R53}.

\subsection{Prompt-Policy Selection Model}

The adaptive component in use is a contextual multi-armed bandit \cite{R86}. This can be viewed as a restricted short-horizon reinforcement-learning setting, but it does not model a long-horizon Markov decision process or estimate a value function over the workflow state space. We nevertheless refer to it as the \emph{RL layer} because that term is used in the implementation and architecture, while its learning scope remains limited.

\subsubsection{Bandit Definition}
Each prompt pack $p_k$ is associated with an arm indexed by $k$ in the stochastic multi-armed bandit \cite{R84}. The arm's success-probability parameter is $\theta_k$, with Beta success and failure soft counts $\alpha_k$ and $\beta_k$. Posterior per arm is:
\begin{equation}
\theta_k \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha_k, \beta_k).
\end{equation}

At each refinement attempt $t$, the context state $\mathbf{h}_t$ determines the permissible arm set $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{safe}}(\mathbf{h}_t)$. The controller proceeds as follows \cite{R85}:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item draw a Thompson sample $\widetilde{\theta}_{k,t}$ from the posterior of each arm in $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{safe}}(\mathbf{h}_t)$,
  \item choose $k_t = \arg\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{safe}}(\mathbf{h}_t)} \widetilde{\theta}_{k,t}$,
  \item apply selected prompt and observe reward proxy,
  \item update $(\alpha_{k_t},\beta_{k_t})$.
\end{enumerate}
The algorithm for updating the posterior distribution is presented in Algorithm~\ref{alg:rl-policy-update}.

Eventually, the system will be able to learn what types of prompts are good for which types of issues. For instance, the prompts for technical-debt type of issues will be short, whereas user-facing type issues will require detailed prompts.

Expected performance of arm $k$:
\begin{equation}
\mathbb{E}[\theta_k] = \frac{\alpha_k}{\alpha_k+\beta_k}.
\end{equation}

The RL layer works with a limited set of prompt-policy arms. An arm is not just a prompt template, but a bounded policy with the definition of the style of decomposition, evidences, validation strictness, and permissible risks. The separation of arms allows one to control more easily the learning activity, as each arm performs its specific functions and takes certain risks. In Table~\ref{tab:policy-arms}, $\bar r_k$ denotes the running mean reward of arm $k$.

In the process of processing each state of the context $\mathbf{h}_t$, a safety filter eliminates the arms that do not meet the criteria of governance, such as sufficient evidences, allowed workflow state, and writable target. The remaining safe arms are defined as:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{safe}}(\mathbf{h}_t)
=
\left\{k\in\mathcal{K}\mid
\chi_j(k,\mathbf{h}_t)=1
\text{ for all }j=1,\ldots,n_{\chi}\right\}.
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathcal{K}$ refers to the entire set of prompt arms, while $\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{safe}}(\mathbf{h}_t)$ refers to the set of arms permissible in attempt $t$. The state $\mathbf{h}_t$ refers to the contextual state used to decide on the arm. The function $\chi_j(k,\mathbf{h}_t)\in\{0,1\}$ checks whether the arm $k$ satisfies governance condition $j$ in the context. The output of 1 represents satisfaction of the condition, while output 0 represents rejection of the arm. Here, $j$ represents a governance condition, while $n_{\chi}$ refers to the total number of conditions. Therefore, an arm is included in the safe set only when the values of all conditions are 1.

Following the above filtering process, the controller uses Thompson sampling technique only for the safe arms. For attempt $t$, the controller draws a value from the Beta posterior of each safe arm and chooses the arm with the highest sampled value:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\widetilde{\theta}_{k,t}
&\sim \operatorname{Beta}(\alpha_k,\beta_k),
&& k\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{safe}}(\mathbf{h}_t),\\
k_t
&=\arg\max_{k\in\mathcal{K}_{\mathrm{safe}}(\mathbf{h}_t)}
\widetilde{\theta}_{k,t}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Therefore, the controller can explore different prompt strategies, but it cannot select an arm that fails a governance condition.

\subsubsection{Confidence-Guided Refinement}

For each refinement attempt $t$, the system records the displayed confidence
$C_{10,t}\in[0,10]$. The remaining gap between this confidence and the target
$\tau_{10}=10\tau_c$ on the displayed scale is calculated as:

\begin{equation}
\ell_t=\max(0,\tau_{10}-C_{10,t}),
\end{equation}

where $\ell_t$ represents the confidence gap. If the target is not reached,
the system uses the latest gap to guide the next refinement attempt. It also
stores the complete confidence trajectory for auditing and debugging. The
refinement process ends when the confidence reaches the target or when the
maximum number of retries is reached.
The complete refinement and writeback workflow is shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:e2e-inference}.

\subsubsection{Laplace-Domain Convergence View}

As the implemented refinement loop operates in a discrete time fashion, its runtime state is encoded in \(\{\ell_t\}_{t=1}^{T}\), where $T$ is the attempt horizon. In order to examine its convergence properties we interpolate the lag trajectory in the form of \(\ell(t)\) and analyze it in the Laplace domain. Such interpolation serves as a diagnostic tool, rather than a substitute for the discrete algorithm, and it is used as a cheap way to verify whether confidence lag decays during iterations.

Let the idealized lag dynamics be:
\begin{equation}
\frac{d\ell(t)}{dt} = -\kappa \ell(t) + d_{\ell}(t),
\end{equation}
where \(\kappa>0\) represents the convergence rate of the chosen prompt policy and \(d_{\ell}(t)\) denotes a bounded disturbance from lack of evidence, conflicting context, or reviewer feedback. Let $s_L$ denote the Laplace-domain variable, $\Lambda_{\ell}(s_L)=\mathcal{L}\{\ell(t)\}$, and $D_{\ell}(s_L)=\mathcal{L}\{d_{\ell}(t)\}$. The transformed dynamics are:
\begin{equation}
s_L\Lambda_{\ell}(s_L)-\ell(0)=-\kappa\Lambda_{\ell}(s_L)+D_{\ell}(s_L),
\end{equation}
and therefore:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_{\ell}(s_L)=\frac{\ell(0)+D_{\ell}(s_L)}{s_L+\kappa}.
\end{equation}
In the no-disturbance case, \(D_{\ell}(s_L)=0\), so:
\begin{equation}
\Lambda_{\ell}(s_L)=\frac{\ell(0)}{s_L+\kappa}.
\end{equation}
The only pole is at \(s_L=-\kappa\). Hence the lag dynamics are stable when \(\kappa>0\). By the final value theorem,
\begin{equation}
\lim_{t\to\infty}\ell(t)=\lim_{s_L\to 0}s_L\Lambda_{\ell}(s_L)=0,
\end{equation}
This implies that confidence lag converges to zero when the refinement policy has positive effective gain and no persistent disturbance. If $|d_{\ell}(t)|\le d_{\max}$, then the steady-state lag magnitude is bounded above by $d_{\max}/\kappa$; hence, larger \(\kappa\) implies faster and tighter convergence.

In practice, the value of \(\kappa\) is estimated from observed attempts using the following formula:
\begin{equation}
\kappa = \max\left(\frac{n}{\sum \delta},\epsilon\right),
\end{equation}
where \(\epsilon>0\) prevents zero denominators in calculations.
The value of \(\kappa\) can also be recorded along with the confidence trajectory and may provide insight into whether another attempt will help.

\subsubsection{Algorithmic Realization}
Algorithms~\ref{alg:e2e-inference} and~\ref{alg:rl-policy-update}
illustrate the described approach in practice.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:e2e-inference} illustrates the full process flow.
It involves obtaining context and generating candidate stories.
It picks a safe prompt arm and improves each story.
A story can be submitted to Jira only after passing the validation, policy, and human review steps.

Algorithm~\ref{alg:rl-policy-update} illustrates the prompt-policy update process.
It calculates a signed reward based on the change in confidence level, reviewer decision, and write back flag.
The reward is mapped to success credit and updated in the selected arm's Beta posterior distribution.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\DontPrintSemicolon
\footnotesize
\caption{End-to-End Governed Epic-to-Story Inference Pipeline}
\label{alg:e2e-inference}
\KwIn{Epic $E$, connectors $\mathcal{X}$ (Jira/Confluence/Bitbucket), policy state $\mathcal{P}$, creation threshold $\tau_c$, escalation threshold $\tau_e$, max attempts $T$}
\KwOut{Created story set $S_{\mathrm{create}}$, audit trace $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{audit}}$}
\tcp{Retrieve available context, scale the threshold, and initialize the trace.}
$\tau_{10}\leftarrow 10\tau_c$; $\mathcal{C} \leftarrow$ RetrieveContext$(E,\mathcal{X})$; $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{audit}}\leftarrow\emptyset$\;
$S_{\mathrm{cand}} \leftarrow$ GenerateCandidates$(E,\mathcal{C})$\;
\tcp{For each candidate, select a governance-safe arm, enrich, rescore, and validate.}
\ForEach{$s \in S_{\mathrm{cand}}$}{
  $t \leftarrow 1$; $\ell_0 \leftarrow \tau_{10}$; $A_s \leftarrow 0$\;
  \While{$t \le T$}{
    $k_t \leftarrow$ SelectPromptArm$(\mathcal{P}, s, \mathcal{C})$\;
    $s \leftarrow$ EnrichWithPrompt$(s,k_t,\mathcal{C})$\;
    $(R_s,C_s) \leftarrow$ ScoreReadinessConfidence$(s,\mathcal{C})$\; \tcp*[r]{$R_s=R(s)$, $C_s=C(s)$}
    $C_{10,t}\leftarrow 10C_s$; $\ell_t \leftarrow \max(0,\tau_{10}-C_{10,t})$\;
    $\widehat{\kappa}_t \leftarrow$ EstimateLaplaceRate$(\ell_{t-1},\ell_t)$\; \tcp*[r]{Attempt-level rate estimate; diagnostic only}
    $V_s \leftarrow$ ConsistencyValidate$(s)$\; \tcp*[r]{$V_s=V(s)$}
    LogAttempt$(\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{audit}},E,s,t,k_t,R_s,C_s,\ell_t,\widehat{\kappa}_t,V_s)$\;
    \If{$(C_s \ge \tau_c)$ \textbf{and} $(V_s=1)$}{\textbf{break}}
    $t \leftarrow t + 1$\;
  }
  \tcp{Determine escalation; policy and explicit human approval remain separate gates.}
  \tcp{$I_{\mathrm{ctx}}(s)$ indicates limited external context; $I_{\mathrm{esc}}(s)$ indicates required escalation.}
  $I_{\mathrm{esc}}(s) \leftarrow \mathbb{1}[C_s < \tau_e] \lor I_{\mathrm{ctx}}(s)$\;
  $(g_{\Pi}(s),A_s) \leftarrow$ PolicyAndHumanGate$(s,I_{\mathrm{esc}}(s))$\; \tcp*[r]{$g_{\Pi}(s)$ indicates policy permission}
  $I_{\mathrm{create}}(s) \leftarrow \mathbb{1}[C_s \ge \tau_c \land A_s=1 \land V_s=1 \land g_{\Pi}(s)=1 \land s\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dup}}]$\;
  \If{$I_{\mathrm{create}}(s)=1$}{
    \If{HashCheckAndIdempotencyPass$(s)$}{ \tcp*[r]{Source and duplicate safety}
      Writeback$(s)$; AddToCreateSet$(S_{\mathrm{create}},s)$\;
    }
  }
}
\Return{$(S_{\mathrm{create}},\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{audit}})$}\;
\end{algorithm}

In Algorithm~\ref{alg:rl-policy-update}, $n_k$ counts the recorded outcomes for arm $k$. The values $C^{\mathrm{before}}_{10,t}$ and $C^{\mathrm{after}}_{10,t}$ are its displayed confidence before and after the outcome; $d_t$ is the reviewer decision and $w_t$ is the writeback status. The signed reward $r_t\in[-1,1]$ is converted to fractional success credit $q_t=(r_t+1)/2$.

\begin{algorithm}[H]
\DontPrintSemicolon
\footnotesize
\caption{Prompt Policy Update (Bandit + Governance-Aware Reward)}
\label{alg:rl-policy-update}
\KwIn{Selected-arm state $(\alpha_{k_t},\beta_{k_t},n_{k_t},\bar r_{k_t})$ and recorded outcome $(C^{\mathrm{before}}_{10,t},C^{\mathrm{after}}_{10,t},d_t,w_t)$}
\KwOut{Updated selected-arm state $(\alpha_{k_t},\beta_{k_t},n_{k_t},\bar r_{k_t})$}
\tcp{Build the signed reward from confidence, reviewer decision, and writeback status.}
$r_t \leftarrow (C^{\mathrm{after}}_{10,t}-C^{\mathrm{before}}_{10,t})/10$\;
$r_t \leftarrow r_t+0.2\mathbb{1}[d_t=\texttt{approve}]-0.2\mathbb{1}[d_t\in\{\texttt{reject},\texttt{regenerate}\}]$\;
$r_t \leftarrow r_t+0.1\mathbb{1}[w_t=\texttt{written}]-0.1\mathbb{1}[w_t=\texttt{conflict}]$\;
\tcp{Bound the reward, update the selected arm, and retain audit statistics.}
$r_t \leftarrow \operatorname{clip}(r_t,-1,1)$\;
$q_t \leftarrow (r_t+1)/2$\;
$(\alpha_{k_t},\beta_{k_t}) \leftarrow (\alpha_{k_t}+q_t,\beta_{k_t}+1-q_t)$\;
$n_{k_t} \leftarrow n_{k_t}+1$\;
$\bar r_{k_t} \leftarrow \bar r_{k_t}+(r_t-\bar r_{k_t})/n_{k_t}$\;
\Return{$(\alpha_{k_t},\beta_{k_t},n_{k_t},\bar r_{k_t})$}\;
\end{algorithm}

\subsection{Epic-to-Story Decomposition Method}


\subsubsection{Candidate Generation}
By leveraging epic context and previous stories, the system can produce any required capability archetypes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item UI/interaction story,
  \item API/contract story,
  \item persistence/writeback story,
  \item validation/regression story.
\end{itemize}

This system rejects the archetypes that are already covered in existing summaries via lexical and semantic matching.

This helps avoid the common error of ``story inflation,'' where assistants create tickets that differ in wording but cover the same work.

In the current implementation, the system recognizes any embedded epic description as a first-class piece of evidence. When an epic is filled with design signals such as solution-design headings, scaling, formulas, APIs and workflows, validation, roll-back considerations, and testing considerations, the system will output a \texttt{jira\_embedded\_context} piece of evidence. This makes the decomposition resilient to the common enterprise challenge where Confluence links are unavailable at the time of execution, while the epic still contains enough design information for bounded planning. In case when the epic has only links and those links are not available, the system avoids the generation of any placeholders and returns the context unavailability message.

\subsubsection{Story Point Assignment}
Initial estimates are derived from complexity priors:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{SP}(s) = f(\xi_{\text{scope}}, \xi_{\text{integration}}, \xi_{\text{risk}}, \xi_{\text{unknown}}),
\end{equation}
with the four $\xi$ factors representing normalized priors for the complexity of scope, integration, risk, and uncertainty. The resulting measure is categorized using Fibonacci-like buckets of 3, 5, and 8. In the current implementation, the default values are deterministic (3/5) and may be learned in future implementations.

Deterministic baseline is an intended choice. It guarantees consistent behavior in initial phases of use and avoids estimation variability until there is enough history to learn from. It also represents a conservative approach to evaluation whereby the value brought in by automation is compared with deterministic baseline performance before applying any other estimation techniques.

\subsubsection{Iterative Enrichment}
For each candidate story $s$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item run semantic analysis,
  \item calculate readiness/confidence,
  \item if $C(s)<\tau_c$, provide guidance and repeat the enrichment process within a bounded attempt budget,
  \item provide a structured story payload.
\end{enumerate}

Enrichment increases as each iteration brings explicit feedback from reviewers, such as missed edge cases, unresolved dependencies, or ambiguous non-functional requirements. Thus, the refinement process becomes a collaboration, not just prompting. The distinction is important for successful interaction between human and AI \cite{R56,R57}.

Moreover, in one embodiment, the output of the enrichment process follows a canonical, strongly typed schema:\linebreak
\texttt{problem\_statement}, \texttt{actor\_value}, \texttt{functional\_scope}, \texttt{nfr\_constraints}, \texttt{acceptance\_criteria},\linebreak
\texttt{dependency\_map}, \texttt{risk\_register}, and \texttt{test\_considerations}. The high degree of typing ensures interoperability of the result with tools for reviews, analytics, and policies and removes ambiguity caused by the informal nature of the free-form text.

An analysis of coverage on the epic level can be done through the capability matrix. Its rows correspond to the capability dimensions such as UI, API, data, observability, resiliency, and security, while its columns represent the suggested stories. The system is able to detect undercovered dimensions and generate candidate stories accordingly. It alters the decomposition process from merely generative to constrained generation with clear objectives.

\subsection{Policy and Governance Layer}

The policy and governance layer controls whether a candidate story may move towards Jira creation. It checks confidence, available context, policy rules, validation, and human approval. The AI may generate and refine a story, but it cannot approve the final writeback.
First, the system checks whether a story needs additional human review:
\begin{equation}
I_{\mathrm{esc}}(s)
= \mathbb{1}[C(s) < \tau_e] \lor I_{\mathrm{ctx}}(s).
\end{equation}
where $\tau_e$ is the normalized escalation threshold and $I_{\text{ctx}}(s)$ denotes a partial external-context condition. This predicate cannot substitute the explicit approval indicator $A(s)$. Here, $I_{\mathrm{esc}}(s)\in\{0,1\}$ is the escalation indicator for candidate story $s$. It equals 1 when the confidence is below $\tau_e$ or when external context is incomplete. In this case, the story requires additional human review. Otherwise, it equals 0. Escalation does not replace the mandatory human-approval gate.

The system next checks whether candidate story $s$ is eligible for creation:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
g_{\Pi}(s) ={}& \mathbb{1}[\Pi(s)=\textsc{allow}],\\
I_{\mathrm{create}}(s) ={}&
\mathbb{1}[C(s)\ge\tau_c \land A(s)=1 \land V(s)=1 \land g_{\Pi}(s)=1 \land s\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dup}}],
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Here, $\mathbb{1}[\cdot]$ is an indicator function. It returns 1 when a condition is true and 0 otherwise. The term $\Pi(s)$ is the decision made by the active policy. Therefore, $g_{\Pi}(s)=1$ means that the policy allows the story.

The creation rule contains five gates. First, $C(s)$ must reach the confidence threshold $\tau_c$. Second, $A(s)=1$ confirms human approval. Third, $V(s)=1$ confirms that validation has passed. Fourth, $g_{\Pi}(s)=1$ confirms policy permission. Finally, $s\in\mathcal{F}_{\mathrm{dup}}$ confirms that the story is not a duplicate.

The value $I_{\mathrm{create}}(s)=1$ only when all five gates pass. If any gate fails, the story is not eligible for creation. This value indicates eligibility only. It does not mean that Jira has already been updated. The source-hash and idempotency checks must also pass, as shown in Algorithm~\ref{alg:e2e-inference}. The conflict and target checks are applied by the writeback model described below.

Confidence and human approval are important gates. However, neither gate is sufficient by itself. Validation, policy permission, and duplicate control are also required. This design keeps the final decision with a reviewer and supports appropriate reliance on automation \cite{R56,R75}.

Approval is always attached to a concrete execution run. This way, it can be evaluated in terms of the policy used during the actual execution of the decision, regardless of modifications made to the thresholds afterward. Traceability is important for regulated environments, in which the reproduction of the process is even more important than the outcome itself.

The governance level may also set the approval rules based on the role of the reviewer: changes to the API contract are approved by the domain owner, whereas changes to authentication flow require security approval. Such approval-routing criteria can be represented as policy predicates based on generated metadata and evidence tags.

\subsection{Conflict-Safe Writeback Model}

The previous section decides whether a candidate may enter the creation stage. It does not make the final change in Jira. In this section, \emph{writeback} means that final Jira change. Approval and writeback may happen at different times. During this gap, the source issue, draft, policy, or target may change. A request may also be retried. The model below blocks stale changes and prevents duplicate creation.

We use two protection levels in this section. A2 combines a source-hash check with atomic idempotency. A3 adds a source-bound transaction envelope. These arrangements are evaluated later in Table~\ref{tab:mutation-safety-ablation}.

\paragraph{Approval-bound context.}
An approval flag records the reviewer's decision. However, it does not record the exact context that was approved. A3 therefore stores this context in an approved transaction envelope, denoted by $\mathcal{E}_a$:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{E}_a=(r_{\mathrm{run}},i_{\mathrm{issue}},h_s,\nu_s,\nu_d,h_d,\nu_{\Pi},h_{\Pi},\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{target}},a_{\mathrm{dec}},u_{\mathrm{rev}},t_a),
\end{equation}
where $r_{\mathrm{run}}$ identifies the run and $i_{\mathrm{issue}}$ identifies the issue. The pair $(h_s,\nu_s)$ records the approved source hash and revision. The pair $(\nu_d,h_d)$ records the approved draft version and payload hash. The pair $(\nu_{\Pi},h_{\Pi})$ records the policy version and snapshot hash. The set $\mathcal{T}_{\mathrm{target}}$ lists the allowed Jira targets. Finally, $a_{\mathrm{dec}}$, $u_{\mathrm{rev}}$, and $t_a$ record the approval result, reviewer, and time.

The system hashes the complete envelope to obtain the transaction identifier $I_{\mathrm{tx}}=H(\mathcal{E}_a)$, where $H(\cdot)$ is a deterministic cryptographic hash function. The A3 idempotency key then binds this transaction to the issue:
\begin{equation}
K_{A3}=H(I_{\mathrm{tx}},i_{\mathrm{issue}}).
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Runtime validation.}
Immediately before writeback, the validator builds the runtime envelope $\mathcal{E}_t$. It has the same fields as $\mathcal{E}_a$. The validator rereads the current source, draft, policy, and target values. It carries forward the run, issue, and approval metadata from the approved envelope.

The system permits a mutation only when the two envelopes match and the decision is still \textsc{approve}:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{permit}(\mathcal{E}_a,\mathcal{E}_t)
=\mathbb{1}[\mathcal{E}_a=\mathcal{E}_t \land a_{\mathrm{dec}}=\textsc{approve}].
\end{equation}
An ABA change occurs when the source moves from state A to B and later returns to the same visible content A. Its revision has still changed. The envelope comparison detects this case. It also detects draft regeneration, policy drift, and target drift. A matching Jira text hash alone is therefore not sufficient.

\paragraph{Source-conflict check.}
The system also calculates a hash of the current normalized issue state:
\begin{equation}
h_{\text{current}} = H(\text{normalized issue state}),
\end{equation}
It compares this value with the approved source hash $h_{\mathrm{expected}}:=h_s$. A mismatch moves the item to the conflict path, and Jira is not changed. Here, $\operatorname{write}(s)=1$ means that story $s$ is written to Jira. Within the declared validator and fault model, the write authorization rule is:
\begin{equation}
\Pr(\text{write}(s)=1 \mid h_{\text{current}}\ne h_{\text{expected}})=0.
\end{equation}

\paragraph{Retry protection.}
While the source check protects from a stale write, repeated requests might cause duplicates. A2 addresses this issue by introducing a payload-level idempotency key:
\begin{equation}
K_{A2} = H(\text{normalized payload},\;\text{target epic},\;\text{policy version}).
\end{equation}
Requests with the same normalized payload, target epic, and policy version will have the same A2 key. A3 employs the envelope-level key mentioned above. Since $I_{\mathrm{tx}}=H(\mathcal{E}_a)$, its key is equivalently $H(H(\mathcal{E}_a),i_{\mathrm{issue}})$. This binds the retry to the approved context.

The key is verified and reserved atomically before mutation. The hashing alone is not enough since deterministic field ordering makes sure that two logically equivalent payloads have the same hash input. Thus, the request may be treated as the same operation and not a new one. In case the approved state was changed, the story should be revalidated and reapproved. All guarantees hold for the implementation and fault model discussed in Table~\ref{tab:mutation-safety-ablation}.

\paragraph{Per-story and batch outcomes.}
The previous rules decide whether one story may be written. A run may have several candidate stories, so the system logs each outcome individually. Let $o_s$ denote the outcome of candidate $s$, where
\[
o_s\in\{\textsc{created},\textsc{already\_exists},\textsc{not\_approved},\textsc{create\_failed}\}.
\]
For a candidate batch $\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{batch}}$, the overall status follows this precedence rule:
\begin{equation}
\operatorname{status}(\mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{batch}})=
\begin{cases}
\textsc{created}, & \exists s\in \mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{batch}}: o_s=\textsc{created},\\
\textsc{failed}, & \exists s\in \mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{batch}}: o_s=\textsc{create\_failed},\\
\textsc{already\_exists}, & \exists s\in \mathcal{B}_{\mathrm{batch}}: o_s=\textsc{already\_exists},\\
\textsc{failed}, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
The rules are processed from top to bottom. A success in the creation has priority. If nothing has been created, the creation error leads to \textsc{failed}. If neither of these conditions is true, the duplicate leads to \textsc{already\_exists}. A batch with only unapproved stories leads to \textsc{failed}. The detailed per-story results remain available in every case. The reviewer can therefore see each skipped item, its reason, any connector error, and the existing Jira key for a duplicate.

To systematically evaluate the performance, governance capabilities, and human interaction profile of Jira Enhancer, our experimental design maps specific empirical evaluations directly to our four research questions.

\subsection{Research Question to Evaluation Mapping}
To provide a structured defense of the system, our evaluations are partitioned into four distinct experimental suites:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Suite 1 (Field Feasibility - RQ1, RQ2):} Evaluates the system's operational viability using live field telemetry from an industrial deployment tracking professional practitioners interacting with actual requirements.
    \item \textbf{Suite 2 (Independent Quality Audit - RQ1, RQ2):} Assesses the absolute generated artifact quality via a blinded evaluation layout to establish an empirical baseline of narrative fitness.

    \item \textbf{Suite 4 (Mutation-Safety Fault Injection - RQ3):} Evaluates the technical resilience of our writeback state guards by intentionally introducing transaction anomalies, API drops, and out-of-order execution schedules.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Blinded Independent Artifact Quality Audit (RQ1, RQ2)}

\noindent\textit{RQ1: How can a human-AI workflow split underspecified software epics into reviewable implementation stories without compromising human authority over acceptance and writeback?}\par

\subsection{Cross-Baseline Comparative Analysis (RQ4)}\label{sec:matched-comparison}
The core baseline evaluation compared the operational characteristics of Jira Enhancer (B3) against a deterministic template rule parser (B1) and a one-shot LLM drafting control (B2). Alongside package quality, the primary evaluation panel (Reviewers 3--5) recorded three outcome parameters: estimated required text modifications, assessment duration, and categorical readiness disposition. These measures assess package review and revision needs, rather than total development efficiency.


\noindent\textit{RQ4: How does a governed human-AI workflow perform, relative to executable template/rule and generic-LLM baselines, on decomposition quality, review effort, and implementation readiness?}\par

For the matched experiment, we held frozen the same 14 de-identified epic and evidence packages for the three methods. Each method produced five stories per package, according to a common schema. B1 ran the deterministic evidence-section parser and candidate builder. When the parser returned fewer than five stories, a declared static completion rule filled the remaining slots; this occurred for 24 of the 70 B1 stories. B2 and B3 passed through the authenticated OpenAI Codex service using the same model configuration, reasoning effort, and evidence limitation. B2 made one generic generation request per epic, without a critic pass, confidence feedback, policy gate, retries, or reviewer fixes. B3 initially planned the epic and subsequently used Jira Enhancer's story-level enrichment with the bounded critic loop, scoring, and policy validation. Human approval and Jira writeback were off for each method.

Resource consumption is part of the treatment definition. B1 made zero model requests. B2 made 14 successful requests, one for each package, while B3 made 173: 14 planning requests and 159 story-enrichment attempts. Summed model-call durations were 2,087 seconds for B2 and 19,906 seconds for B3. These are cumulative invocation times, not elapsed wall-clock times, because inputs were processed in parallel. This comparison evaluates the complete workflows and is not normalized for model resources.

Five reviewers each received an independently randomized workbook containing 42 primary packages and four variants based on masked repetitions. The variants based on the same input element were separated by at least ten rating rows. All workbooks masked method identity, model and runtime information, source IDs, links, confidence, policy status, and authorship. The reviewers scored, using the anchored 1--5 scale, scope correctness, coverage completeness, story uniqueness, acceptance and testability, dependency and risk handling, granularity and executability, and implementation readiness. They also indicated disposition, confidence, actual time spent on assessment, the estimated number of substantial modifications required, and an evidence-related comment. Because Reviewers~1 and~2 had previously seen B3-only material, the prespecified primary analysis uses Reviewers~3--5. The result from all five reviewers is reported as a sensitivity analysis, and masked repeats are excluded from every method-effect estimate.

The current analysis uses the corrected reviewer ratings and a revised priority-weighted composite. Implementation readiness receives 40\%, dependency and risk treatment 20\%, and granularity and actionability 20\%. Scope fidelity, coverage completeness, story distinctness, and acceptance and testability each receive 5\%. These weights total 100\% and retain the anchored 1--5 scale. They were selected after the initial analysis to emphasize implementation needs; the weighted outcome is therefore exploratory. The same weights are applied to every method and reviewer.

We treated the epic as the unit of inference. Weighted scores were averaged across the three primary reviewers within each input-method cell, leaving 14 matched observations per method. We used a 200,000-draw within-epic permutation test for the three-method comparison; exact paired sign-flip tests for B3--B2 and B3--B1; 100,000-draw epic-bootstrap confidence intervals \cite{R97}; Holm correction \cite{R96} across the two contrasts within each outcome family; and paired rank-biserial effect sizes. Review time and edit counts used the same matched procedure. The omnibus weighted-composite difference was detectable (\(Q_{\mathrm{omni}}=23.29\), Monte Carlo \(p<10^{-5}\)).

\subsection{Held-Out Source Fidelity and Readiness Diagnostic (RQ4)}\label{sec:heldout-source-diagnostic}

Our diagnostic examined source fidelity, requirement coverage, and the clarity of implementation criteria in outputs from the learned prompt-selection configuration. It compared one-shot B2 with B3 using governance-filtered arm selection. The model assessments complement the matched human study and are reported separately.

The diagnostic evaluated nine versioned, bounded prompt-policy arms. Each arm represented a refinement strategy. The selected strategy was combined with the current packet evidence and refinement state. One observation for each arm was made on 11 non-held-out training packets. This resulted in 99 critic observations. Learning modified the posterior Beta used for subsequent arm selection. The critic verified four criteria: whether there is a source-grounded gap, the value of the suggested correction, non-redundancy, and grounding accuracy. Three held-out packets did not participate in this process.

Each arm started out with a $\operatorname{Beta}(1,1)$ prior. Bounded critic reward $r\in[-1,1]$ was translated to a fraction of success as $(r+1)/2$. This value updated the posterior of the respective arm. In each held-out refinement iteration, relevant and governed arms became part of the safe-arm set. The controller picked the best performing arm from the rest of the eligible arms based on posterior mean. Exploration was set to zero, and a selected arm was not reused for the same packet. The controller applied exactly three arms to each packet. Ambiguity resolution, failure and recovery, and interface contracts were selected for all three packets. Their order differed for one packet. Figure~\ref{fig:b2-b3-arm-learning} shows the recorded posterior trajectory and final arm weights.

\section{DISCUSSION AND WORKFLOW TRADE-OFFS}\label{sec:discussion}
Our empirical findings provide a bounded answer to the research questions and expose the trade-offs in applying agentic systems to shared engineering records. The central insight is that text fluency must not be conflated with requirement readiness. A single-turn language model can produce useful requirement drafts, but the studied B2 control ends at generation. It does not implement the evidence, approval, recovery, and writeback safeguards provided by B3.

\subsection{Operational Advantages of B3 over One-Shot Drafting}
The utility of a requirement-generation system includes source grounding, implementation readiness, and control over tracker changes. The evaluations identify three advantages of the complete B3 configuration:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Source fidelity and omission findings.} In the held-out diagnostic (Section~\ref{sec:heldout-source-diagnostic}), two blinded model evaluators assessed the same three packets. Their summed findings included 4 unsupported additions and 13 missing source requirements for B2, compared with zero of each for B3. Ambiguous acceptance-criterion findings were 21 for B2 and 4 for B3. Both evaluators preferred B3 on every packet, with combined scores of 187/192 for B3 and 148/192 for B2. These descriptive assessments support stronger source grounding within the diagnostic. They are not human-panel ratings, deduplicated defect counts, or a guarantee that the verification loop will detect every semantic error.
  \item \textbf{Protection against stale and duplicate mutations.} B3's writeback mechanism binds approval to the reviewed source revision, draft, policy, and target set. In Table~\ref{tab:mutation-safety-ablation}, the A1 and A2 source-hash arrangements each retained a 44.4\% stale-mutation rate across their scenario-balanced observations. A3 recorded zero unsafe mutations in its 18,000 observations and permitted all eligible writes. The full A0--A3 suite contains 72,000 observations. These are tests of transaction mechanisms, not evidence that the text-only B2 condition itself caused production failures; Jira writeback was disabled in the matched artifact comparison. The result supports the source-bound execution barrier under the declared fault model.
  \item \textbf{Higher weighted requirement quality and implementation readiness.} B3 scored 4.32 versus 3.59 for B2 under the revised priority weights, a paired difference of 0.74 points ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.00024$). Mean implementation-readiness ratings were 4.60 versus 3.52. Ready-or-Minor-revision ratings were 88.1\% versus 66.7\%, with 5 versus 14 Major-revision ratings; both methods received 18 fully Ready ratings. B3 also reduced recorded assessment time by 2.15 minutes ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.00024$). These outcomes describe the corrected ratings in the matched portfolio and the complete tested workflows.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{The Computational Resource Trade-off}
The operational benefits require additional model work. B2 made 14 model calls, with a cumulative invocation duration of 2,087 seconds. B3 made 173 calls, with a cumulative duration of 19,906 seconds. These totals sum model-call durations; they are not elapsed wall-clock times because inputs were processed in parallel. The comparison therefore does not establish computational efficiency for B3.

The extra model work may be worthwhile where review effort and safe tracker updates are important, but that deployment decision needs measured costs. The study did not record inference charges, financial losses from tracker errors, or downstream delivery savings. It therefore cannot establish that backend costs are negligible or that the trade-off is financially favorable in every organization. The observed trade-off is additional model work alongside higher weighted requirement-quality ratings, lower assessment time, a more favorable revision profile, and the tested mutation safeguards.

\subsection{Answers to the Research Questions}
\textbf{RQ1: Governed decomposition.} The field telemetry and quality audits show that the workflow can decompose corporate epics into reviewable stories while preserving a separate human decision before writeback. Across two deployment waves, 69 stories were created and one existing record was linked through duplicate-safe matching. This establishes successful-path feasibility within the evaluated setting.

\textbf{RQ2: Confidence, evidence, and readiness.} Machine confidence, evidence provenance, and policy checks give reviewers explicit reasons to accept, revise, or reject a candidate. Confidence is optimized by the pipeline itself, so reaching the threshold is not independent evidence of correctness. The source-grounding findings in the model diagnostic and the human-rated revision profile provide separate assessments of requirement quality; human approval remains necessary for domain decisions and external mutation.

\textbf{RQ3: Tracker failure modes.} The fault-injection suite identifies stale-source, draft, policy, and target changes, together with lost-response and concurrent retries. Under the tested fault schedules, simpler controls permitted stale or duplicate mutations. A3 recorded zero unsafe mutations in its 18,000 observations and allowed every eligible write. Human authorization therefore needs to remain tied to the specific source, draft, policy, and target state reviewed by the user. The experiment tests this execution barrier, not the production frequency of faults.

\textbf{RQ4: Quality and workflow trade-offs.} In the revised weighted analysis, B3 exceeded B2 by 0.74 points and B1 by 1.76 points, with higher scores on all 14 matched epics and Holm-adjusted $p=0.00024$ for each contrast. B3 also received higher implementation-readiness ratings, a more favorable revision profile, and a 2.15-minute reduction in recorded assessment time relative to B2. The separate held-out model diagnostic favored B3 on source fidelity, coverage, and implementation criteria. The estimated-edit contrast with B2 remained uncertain (95\% CI [$-1.55$, 0.19], $p=0.205$). These findings concern the complete workflows and the revised exploratory outcome. B3 used more model resources.

\subsection{Reliability and Recovery Implications}
The fault results show why retry handling must preserve the approved decision and distinguish a repeated request from a new mutation. The workflow in Section~\ref{sec:workflow-ui} keeps selected stories and partial outcomes visible so that a reviewer can inspect, retry, revise, or cancel. Reduced-context warnings also make missing evidence visible instead of treating a fluent draft as fully supported. These controls explain how the system supports recovery; their effects on user behavior and production incident rates require further study.

\subsection{Positioning and Novelty Scope}

Evidence blocks can validate various claims: 70 authenticated endpoints show that a successful path is feasible; independent ratings were provided for 69 full-text stories and 14 epic packages; five experts compared 42 B1, B2, and B3 packages together with 210 stories; fault injection shows safety of writeback; 5{,}000-unit and public issue text blocks show declared numerical behavior. The study does not measure cross-project and longitudinal delivery benefits, which remain important in light of unreliability, human values, and unavailability of standardized evaluation of AI in software engineering \cite{R59,R81,R60}.

The implementation includes configurable prompt-arm selection. B3 does not use an unrestricted prompt loop. It organizes refinement strategies as versioned, bounded policy arms. At runtime, the selected strategy is combined with the current evidence, gap signals, confidence, and attempt state. A contextual bandit selects only from arms that pass the governance checks \cite{R84,R86}. Learning updates the Beta posterior used for later selection. The selected arm, refinement result, and posterior update are recorded for auditing, as shown in Table~\ref{tab:policy-arms} and Algorithm~\ref{alg:rl-policy-update}. This design supports adaptive selection without giving the learning layer permission to modify Jira. Final mutation still requires validation, policy checks, and human approval.

The matched human study used Thompson sampling over a frozen pre-study posterior as a controlled evaluation configuration. Posterior updates were disabled during artifact generation to prevent reviewer outcomes from influencing later artifacts. Prompt-arm selection, bounded refinement, critic checks, and policy evaluation remained active. Freezing the posterior therefore did not turn off arm selection or eliminate sampling variability. This was a comparison of complete workflows, not a component ablation: B2 and B3 used a common output schema, and the experiment did not isolate the schema, critic, or learning policy's contribution.

The subsequent diagnostic (Section~\ref{sec:heldout-source-diagnostic}) separately recorded 99 offline critic observations across 11 non-held-out packets, excluding the three evaluation packets. Figure~\ref{fig:b2-b3-arm-learning} shows the posterior updates; the held-out stage then used governance-filtered, zero-exploration selection. These records make learning and selection auditable, but they do not establish statistical convergence or isolate the benefit of learning. Together, the two evaluations support workflow-level findings and inspection of the bandit mechanism. They do not measure live human interaction with an updating policy. A matched comparison against fixed or rule-based arm selection is needed to estimate learning's incremental value.

B3 controls learning, confidence, governance, approval, recovery, and conflict-free writeback. The score of B3's blinded artifact did not differ significantly from B2's, but B3 took less assessment time, had a descriptively better revision profile, and was the only one that could proceed through a source-bound mutation pathway tested under stale state, retry, draft drift, policy drift, and target drift. This evidence supports the bounded advantage of end-to-end workflows.

\section{Lessons Learned}\label{sec:lessons}

The study produced six practical lessons. Each lesson is limited to the evidence that supports it.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Different evaluations answer different questions.} The study uses four types of evidence, as summarized in Table~\ref{tab:evidence-hierarchy}. First, the field trace contains 70 verified end states. These include 69 newly created Jira issues and one safe-duplicate resolution. The latest wave also includes 44 audited new payloads. Second, human reviewers assessed artifact quality. Two reviewers assessed 69 full-text stories and 14 epic packages. Five reviewers compared 42 matched B1, B2, and B3 packages containing 210 stories. Third, the fault-injection experiment tested mutation safety across 72,000 scenario-balanced observations. Finally, formula-based sensitivity analyses remain diagnostic evidence. These evaluations have different goals and units. Therefore, their results are reported separately and are not combined into one overall result.

  \item \textbf{B3 automates refinement that B2 leaves outside the pipeline.} B2 generated one generic version per epic and then terminated. There was no critic step, confidence feedback, retry process, or prompt policy choice. Further refinement needed human guidance or another controller. B3 employed a bounded critic loop with learned, governance-filtered prompt arm selection. In the parallel experiment, the average assessment time was 2.15 minutes shorter with B3. The reviewers made 5 Major revision choices for B3 and 14 for B2. The revised weighted requirement-quality score was 0.74 points higher for B3 (Holm-adjusted $p=0.00024$). In the subsequent held-out diagnostic test, the model assessors proposed 40 further prompts for B3 and 50 for B2. They also preferred B3 on all six comparison judgments. These data favor automatic, governed refinement with less follow-up. They do not indicate better prose or reduced compute cost overall. Human judgment is required for omitted domain choices and final approval.

  \item \textbf{Human approval requires a technical limit.} Simply approving the draft cannot prevent the mutation from being stale or redundant. The approved draft must stay tied to the source state that was reviewed by the human. This is accomplished through the source-tied transaction envelope. During the fault injection experiment, it prevented all injected dangerous mutations and permitted all legitimate writes, as noted in Table~\ref{tab:mutation-safety-ablation}. Human power is greater when the writeback contract enforces the approval decision.

  \item \textbf{Missing evidence must be made known.} The system can proceed in reduced context when there is no external evidence. It cannot hide the lack of source evidence. The warning codes and confidence provenance will assist the human in understanding this problem. The draft can still be valuable without being complete evidence.

  \item \textbf{The recovery process should maintain human control.} The generation and write-back procedures can fail at any stage. The workflow should enable the reviewer to continue, try again, or cancel. Regeneration without human interaction would compromise control and auditing.

  \item \textbf{Adaptive models require full trajectories and cost proof.} The live data have the terminal states but not each of the refinement attempts. They cannot prove a complete live learning trajectory. In the latter, there were 99 observations from an offline critic. Those observations revealed how the posterior probabilities evolved but they were model-generated data and not human and delivery outcomes. In comparison to B2, B3 did more model work in both comparisons. Hence faster package assessment does not mean lower cost of computation. Future studies should have each attempt recorded and the outcomes normalized with respect to cost.
\end{enumerate}

Collectively, these lessons lead to a modest conclusion. B3 shows an established path from data to an approved mutation based on the analyzed conditions. The paper does not demonstrate a better prose, delivery quality, productivity, computing efficiency or generalization over different projects.

\section{IMPLICATIONS AND THREATS TO VALIDITY}\label{sec:implications}
Our findings carry practical implications for engineering organizations: generative AI assistants should be deployed as governed workflows, with explicit review and controlled tracker updates. Teams should assess source fidelity, missing requirements, acceptance-criteria clarity, and revision readiness separately from prose fluency and the system's internal confidence score. A draft can be useful while still needing domain clarification. Before any external write, approval should remain bound to the reviewed state, and recovery should preserve the reviewer's decisions.

The evaluations also expose a resource trade-off. The matched B3 workflow used 173 model calls, compared with 14 for B2. Lower human assessment time therefore does not establish lower total computational cost. Organizations should measure both model resources and subsequent engineering outcomes when deciding where governed refinement is useful.

\subsection{Study Scope and Validity}
Following the validity classification described by Wohlin et al.~\cite{R55}, we assess internal, construct, external, and conclusion validity.
\textbf{Internal validity:} this concerns whether the observed differences come from the evaluated methods rather than the study design. We used the same inputs, neutral formatting, independent randomization, and blinding to reduce bias \cite{R55}. However, the matched workflows were not compute-matched. B2 used 14 model calls, while B3 used 173. For B1, the static completion rule filled slots for 24 of 70 stories. Reviewers~1 and~2 had previously seen B3-only material. We therefore excluded them from the primary analysis. The later held-out diagnostic also compared unequal configurations. B2 used one pass, while B3 used three refinement passes after its initial draft. Their model configurations and reasoning settings also differed. B3 was 67\% longer. The diagnostic therefore cannot isolate the effect of learned selection. The generated-unit analysis also uses formulas rather than executed treatments. It cannot estimate a treatment effect \cite{R71}.

\textbf{Construct validity:} this concerns whether the measures represent the concepts claimed in the study. Confidence is produced and optimized by the same pipeline. It is therefore not an independent measure of quality. Human approval records a workflow decision, not later delivery success. Blinded reviewer ratings measure visible content quality. Recorded assessment time is not total planning time, and the edit counts are estimates. Reviewers could not see the governance metadata. Therefore, the artifact score does not measure policy or writeback value.

\noindent\textbf{Human Factors and Interactive Limits.} Because our evaluation design prioritizes artifact-level actionability, writeback transaction integrity, and total manual review duration, it does not explicitly log standard human-computer interaction (HCI) parameters such as NASA-TLX cognitive workload indices, System Usability Scale (SUS) scores, or real-time user fatigue variables over multi-sprint intervals. While our efficiency data confirm a significant 2.15-minute reduction in assessment time, this metric tracks out-of-band validation behavior rather than real-time interaction patterns inside a live console interface. Consequently, our findings establish lower requirement evaluation friction without claiming optimal interface layout ergonomics, calibrated user trust balance, or long-horizon psychological adoption rates.

\textbf{External validity:} this concerns whether the findings can be applied to other settings. The 70 endpoints come from 14 epics, two deployment waves, and one organization. They represent successful paths only. The data contain no rejected, abandoned, below-threshold, or conflict endpoint. The matched study uses the same portfolio and has no independently authored manual baseline. The learned-selection diagnostic uses only three purposively selected packets and two model evaluators. The public issue-text analysis also does not execute the methods. Claims about transfer therefore require new projects, domains, and practitioners. This is especially important for context-dependent socio-technical tools \cite{R71,R58}.

\textbf{Conclusion validity:} this concerns the strength and precision of the conclusions. The epic-level exact and cluster-resampled analyses use 14 matched epic clusters. The current analysis uses corrected reviewer ratings and weights chosen after the initial analysis, so the weighted findings are exploratory. Holm adjustment covers the declared contrast families; independent replication with the weights fixed in advance would assess their stability on new inputs. Reviewer agreement and the four repeated items per reviewer describe consistency in this portfolio. The held-out diagnostic has six paired model judgments and is descriptive, as are the disposition counts. Fault-injection rates describe the tested scenario mixture.

\textbf{Reference currency:} research on human--AI collaboration in software engineering is developing quickly. The related literature should therefore be reviewed and updated periodically \cite{R72}.

\textbf{Mitigation:} future studies should use independently prepared manual packages and compute-matched B2 and B3 configurations. The blinded study should be repeated on new portfolios, and reviewer expertise levels should be recorded. Ablation tests should disable the critic, retrieval, validation, and policy layers one at a time. Future work should also test sensitivity to the selected weights and assess the practical significance of any change. Finally, later studies should observe edits, failures, rework, and cycle-time variation across multiple releases.

\subsection{Artifact and Confidentiality Boundary}\label{sec:artifact-access}

The review artifact retains deterministic analysis scripts, de-identified telemetry for the 70 story endpoints, a de-identified audit of the latest-wave payloads, coded ratings from both artifact assessments, aggregate matched-test tables, fault-injection trial records, seeds, and checksums. The shareable data exclude issue and epic keys, internal URLs, account names, email addresses, and timestamps. Raw Jira and Confluence records, the sanitized matched-input packets, and the private method-allocation key remain access-controlled because the source requirements are confidential enterprise material. We did not collect reviewer names, demographic variables, sensitive attributes, or other personal identifiers. The study also does not measure trust, workload, satisfaction, or individual performance.

\subsection{Future Extensions}

Further research may advance the work of Jira Enhancer in five dimensions. First, multi-objective reinforcement learning can find a trade-off between the confidence level and the cost of models. Second, project velocity will allow story-point estimation. Third, active learning can discover the stories that should be manually verified. Fourth, multilingual prompts will enable the work of the teams using different languages. Finally, dependency graph will link Jira issues, documents, code, and incidents. This graph will help the system to locate the related work and critical paths before decomposition of an epic.

\section{CONCLUSION}\label{sec:conclusion}
This paper addresses a critical challenge in agentic software engineering: how to leverage generative AI to decompose incomplete corporate epics into execution-ready stories without sacrificing tracking control, accountability, or data integrity. We presented Jira Enhancer, an operational framework that wraps generative model calls inside programmatic policy gates, machine confidence metrics, and conflict-safe writeback transactions.

The industrial deployment involved a team of 40 professional practitioners across 14 epics and established successful-path feasibility. The main quality findings concern source grounding and revision readiness. In the three-packet held-out diagnostic, two model evaluators recorded no unsupported additions or missing requirements for B3, compared with 4 and 13 findings for B2; ambiguous acceptance-criterion findings were 4 versus 21. B3 scored 187/192 versus 148/192, and both evaluators preferred it on every packet. These are descriptive model assessments, not an independently verified accuracy rate or an isolated estimate of the benefit of learning.

In the matched human study, the revised weighted analysis gave B3 a mean score of 4.32, compared with 3.59 for B2 and 2.56 for B1. B3 scored higher than both baselines on all 14 epics. The B3--B2 paired difference was 0.74 points (95\% CI [0.55, 0.93], Holm-adjusted $p=0.00024$). B3 received 88.1\% Ready-or-Minor-revision ratings compared with 66.7\% for B2, and 5 Major-revision ratings compared with 14. Both received 18 fully Ready ratings. B3 assessment time was 2.15 minutes lower, while the required-edit interval included zero. The weights were revised after the initial analysis, and the reported comparison is exploratory. B3 used additional model resources to provide these workflow capabilities.

The source-bound transaction envelope recorded zero unsafe mutations and permitted all eligible writes in A3's 18,000 fault-test observations. This result is bounded by the tested implementation and fault schedules. The practical takeaway is to combine source-grounded refinement and visible revision needs with an enforceable approval boundary; fluent text or high internal confidence alone is insufficient authorization for a tracker update. Broader claims about delivery quality, productivity, or cross-project transfer require further evaluation.

To support replication, the implementation, analysis scripts, and shareable evidence are available at \url{https://github.com/manish94u/JiraEnhancer}. The repository includes de-identified field tables and aggregate evaluation records. Confidential source requirements and restricted records remain subject to the access boundary described in Section~\ref{sec:artifact-access}.
